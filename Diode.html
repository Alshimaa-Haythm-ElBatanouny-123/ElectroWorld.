Diode



A diode is an electronic component that allows current to flow in only one direction.
It is made from a semiconductor material, typically silicon or germanium, and has two terminals:
Anode (+): The positive terminal.
Cathode (-): The negative terminal.

How a Diode Works:

1. Forward Bias (Conducting Mode):
When the anode is connected to a positive voltage relative to the cathode, the diode allows current to flow.
The voltage required to "turn on" the diode is called the forward voltage (e.g., ~0.7V for silicon diodes).

2. Reverse Bias (Blocking Mode):
When the anode is connected to a negative voltage relative to the cathode, the diode blocks the current, acting as an insulator.
If the reverse voltage exceeds a certain limit (called the breakdown voltage)
The diode can be damaged unless it is designed for such conditions (e.g., Zener diodes).

Types of Diodes:


1. Standard Diodes:
For general-purpose rectification.

2. Zener Diodes:
Designed to allow reverse current at a specific breakdown voltage (used for voltage regulation).

3. Light Emitting Diodes (LEDs):
Emit light when current flows through them.

4. Schottky Diodes:
Have a low forward voltage drop and are used for high-speed switching.

5. Photodiodes:
Generate current when exposed to light.

Applications of Diodes:

1. Rectification:
Converting AC to DC in power supplies.

2. Signal Clipping and Clamping:
Limiting voltage levels in circuits.

3. Voltage Regulation:
Using Zener diodes to maintain a stable output voltage.

4. Protection Circuits:
Preventing reverse current flow to protect sensitive components.

5. Light Emission:
LEDs are widely used in indicators, displays, and lighting.

Practical Example:

In a rectifier circuit, a diode is used to convert an alternating current (AC) signal to a direct current
(DC) signal by allowing current to flow only during the positive half-cycles of the AC waveform.
