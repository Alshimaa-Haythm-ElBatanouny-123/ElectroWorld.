Capacitor


A capacitor is an electronic component that stores and releases electrical energy in a circuit.
It consists of two conductive plates separated by an insulating material called the dielectric.
Capacitors are measured in farads (F), which indicate their ability to store charge.

How a Capacitor Works

1. When a voltage is applied across the plates, the capacitor stores energy in the form of an electric field.

2. The amount of charge a capacitor can store depends on:
* The surface area of the plates.
* The distance between the plates.
* The properties of the dielectric material.

3. Capacitors block direct current (DC) but allow alternating current (AC) to pass, depending on the frequency.

Key Properties

Capacitance ():
The ability to store charge, measured in farads.

Voltage Rating:
The maximum voltage the capacitor can withstand without breaking down.

Charge and Discharge Time:
Determined by the resistor-capacitor (RC) time constant in the circuit.

Types of Capacitors

1. Electrolytic Capacitors:
High capacitance, used in power supplies and filtering.

2. Ceramic Capacitors:
Small and versatile, used in high-frequency applications.

3. Tantalum Capacitors:
Stable and reliable for low-voltage circuits.

4. Film Capacitors:
Durable, used in audio and power systems.

5. Supercapacitors:
Extremely high capacitance for energy storage.

Applications of Capacitors

1. Energy Storage:
Used in power supplies to smooth out fluctuations.

2. Signal Filtering:
Blocks DC signals while allowing AC signals to pass.

3. Timing Circuits:
Creates time delays in oscillators and timers.

4. Coupling and Decoupling:
Transfers signals between circuit stages and isolates noise.

5. Power Factor Correction:
Improves efficiency in electrical systems.

Practical Example

In a power supply, a capacitor is connected parallel to the output to reduce ripples in the DC voltage,
ensuring a steady output for sensitive electronic devices.
